Classic TSP must visit every shop, which is impossible in one shift. It only minimises distance, and the shortest route can arrive when shops will not accept. And it has no concept of time at all.
New TSP variant

Orienteering with time windows: choose which shops, not only the order

INPUT

Depot and n shops, complete distance matrix, one window [opens, closes] per shop, departure 06:00, deadline 24:00, speed 40 km/h

OUTPUT

A route such as [0, 3, 1, 0]: an ordered subset of shops that starts and ends at the depot

CONSTRAINTS

Early arrival waits. Late arrival is forbidden. Each shop at most once. Depot only at the two ends. Back by the deadline.

SCORING

1. More places visited wins. 2. On a tie, shorter distance wins.
Invalid, crashed or timed out is a failure, never a silent zero.

In the literature this is the Orienteering Problem with Time Windows, a prize-collecting TSP. Every place is worth one point. Selecting which cities to serve is part of the problem, which is the fundamental change from TSP.

Slot each shop into the gap that adds the least distance

  

    

    

      

      

      

      

    

  

  
Unlike nearest neighbour, insertion can place a shop between two existing stops, so the route is reshaped rather than only extended. Each round tries every unvisited customer in every gap. A customer that fits nowhere this round can never fit later, so it is pruned for good. Press Enter to watch the rounds on the same four-shop instance.

Approach B · iterated local search (ILS)
Destroy part of the route, rebuild it, keep the best ever seen

  

    

    

      

      

      

      

      

      
Real instance n5-seed17 from our run. The final route matches the saved harness result.

    

  

  
The destroy step lets the search escape a local optimum, a route that no single greedy insertion can improve even though a better one exists. Press Enter to watch a real five-shop instance. The time-shift build already reaches 4 visits, where GIC reaches 3. Then the shake removes customer 3, and the refill brings in customer 4: the same 4 visits on a shorter route, so it becomes the new best. While the search is stuck, the window sweeps along the route and then widens; a new best resets it. Failed shakes are not undone immediately: the walk continues from the worse route and returns to the best after 150 failures.







  
ILS · what makes it different
Three design choices, driven by time being the scarce resource

  

    
1 · TIME SHIFT
Score an insertion by how much it pushes the schedule later, including new waiting.
Ties: added distance, then earlier closing, then id.

    
2 · FORWARD SLACK
max_shift[p] = min(
 closes[p] − visit[p],
 waiting[p+1] + max_shift[p+1])
Feasible if shift ≤ waiting[p] + max_shift[p]: O(1) again. Waiting absorbs delay first.

    
3 · DETERMINISTIC SHAKE
No random numbers: the same input always gives the same output.
Stops at 4,000 stalled iterations or 2,000,000 candidate checks.

  

  
Follows Vansteenwegen et al. (2009), ILS for the team orienteering problem with time windows. With every place worth 1, its ratio score²/shift reduces to smallest shift.

  
If asked whether ILS is just greedy plus extra time: it has a different insertion criterion, time rather than distance; a different slack structure; and a different search paradigm that escapes local optima. Every candidate best is replayed with the validator's arithmetic before being saved.

Interesting findings
What the experiments taught us

  

  
Six findings. The most useful: time, not distance, is the bottleneck. ILS's switch to least time shift is the main reason it beats GIC. Note the budget: ILS uses its full 2 million checks from about n=50, its lead peaks near n=100 and then shrinks by n=300.







  
Trade-offs and limitations
What these results do not claim

  

    
THE MODEL IS SIMPLIFIED
  • Constant speed, no traffic
  • Straight-line distance, not roads
  • Zero service time at each stop
  • One window per place, one vehicle
  • Windows random, not real shop hours

    
THE EVIDENCE HAS LIMITS
  • No proof of optimality: no exact solver, so ILS is "best found", not "optimal"
  • Runtime is measured in Python, including process start
  • ILS trades runtime for quality, and at large n its fixed budget limits that quality

  

  
We state these plainly. In particular, percent of best found compares our three algorithms with each other. Since we dropped branch and bound, we cannot say how far ILS is from the true optimum.







  
X-factor
Our edge is how rigorously the claims can be checked

  

    
EVALUATION METHODOLOGY
Independent validator, paired seeded instances and bootstrap CIs.
Why it counts: a score cannot be misreported, and differences come with uncertainty.

    
O(1) FEASIBILITY CACHE
With an exactness argument and a floating-point guard.
Why it counts: 7.5× faster at n=100 with identical routes, so zero quality cost.

    
DETERMINISTIC ILS
A systematic shake and a work budget, not a clock.
Why it counts: same input, same output, and machine speed cannot change the result.

    
"UNTIL EVERY PLACE IS VISITED"
A second, multi-day metric: days and working hours to finish all shops.
Why it counts: it matches "leftovers go tomorrow".
Clearing 100 shops takes 7.7 days with ILS, 8.6 with GIC, 15.0 with NN.

  

  
The brief says a fancy UI is not an X-factor, so we do not claim our website as one. We claim the methodology, the exact cache, the reproducible search, and the second metric that matches the real use case. Over 10 instances of 100 shops, working hours to finish everything average 124.6 with ILS, 136.2 with GIC and 202.2 with nearest neighbour.
